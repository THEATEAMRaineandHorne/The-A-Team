Selling an Older House in Avondale When New Townhouses Are Going Up Nearby

New townhouses have changed many Avondale streets. If you own an older home nearby, here is how buyers are likely to compare it with the new builds, and how to sell it well.

By Edita Andrijasevic · Published 26 October 2026 · Updated 26 October 2026

Many Avondale streets have changed in recent years. Older standalone homes now sit next to new terraced and townhouse developments, and more are being built. If you own an older home in Avondale, it is natural to wonder how the new builds will affect your sale.

New townhouses do change the comparison buyers make, but they do not make an older home harder to sell by default. This guide looks at how buyers compare older homes with new builds in Avondale, and how to price and present your home for that comparison. For local selling support, see the Avondale real estate agent service page.

The quick answer

An older Avondale home should not try to compete with new townhouses on being new. It sells best when it is priced against recent standalone home sales, and marketed on what townhouses usually cannot offer: land, privacy, a garden, parking, character and room to improve or develop. Being upfront about any construction nearby builds buyer confidence.

How new townhouses change the buyer comparison

When buyers look at Avondale, many will view both new townhouses and older homes. The new builds offer things an older home cannot: new kitchens and bathrooms, modern insulation, low maintenance and the confidence of new construction. Some buyers will always choose those.

Other buyers want something different. They are looking for land, a private garden, space for children or pets, room to extend, a garage, or simply a standalone home with no shared walls. For these buyers, a well-presented older home can be the better choice, and the more townhouses there are, the more distinctive a standalone home on its own section can become.

Knowing which buyers your home suits best helps decide how it should be marketed.

FamiliesWanting outdoor space, separate living areas and no shared walls, with the train and town centre nearby.
RenovatorsLooking for a sound older home, often a bungalow or character home, that they can improve over time.
Developers and buildersInterested in sites where the land, zoning and access may suit new housing.
InvestorsWeighing land and rental demand from Avondale's train and town-centre access.

Price against the right sales

The most common pricing mistake is mixing up townhouse and standalone home sales. A new townhouse and an older home on its own section usually sit in different buyer pools, and their prices move differently. The most relevant evidence for an older home is recent sales of similar standalone homes in Avondale: similar land, condition, title and location.

Buyers will still look at what a new townhouse costs, so it helps to know how your home compares. If an older home is priced well above what a buyer could pay for a new townhouse nearby, the price needs to be clearly explained by the land, space or potential that comes with it. Market what new builds cannot offer

An older home will not win on being new, so the campaign should not try. Instead, make clear what it offers that townhouses nearby usually do not:

  • Land and outdoor space. Show the size and usability of the section, the garden, and where children, pets or vegetable gardens fit.
  • Privacy and separation. No shared walls, fewer neighbours close by and, where it applies, a quieter rear position.
  • Parking and storage. A garage, carport or off-street parking can matter to buyers comparing homes with limited parking.
  • Character. Bungalows and older homes often have features buyers cannot find in new builds, such as high ceilings, timber floors or established trees.
  • Room to improve. For renovators, the upside is part of the appeal, so show it honestly rather than hiding the home's age.

Older homes do need to feel cared for. Fixing obvious maintenance, cleaning thoroughly and tidying the section help buyers compare your home fairly with new builds. Edita's home presentation advice focuses on the changes most likely to matter.

Handling construction next door

If building work is happening next door or nearby, buyers will see it, and many will hear it. They may also wonder how the finished buildings will affect sunlight, privacy, outlook and parking on the street.

  • Be upfront. Tell your agent what you know about the work next door. Many buyers check council records for nearby resource consents, so it is better to explain it early than to have buyers discover it themselves.
  • Show the finished picture where you can. If plans or renders are publicly available, they can help buyers understand what is being built.
  • Choose viewing times carefully. Open homes at times when the site is quiet, often at weekends, let buyers see the home as it is to live in.
  • Present the home calmly. Good light, tidy outdoor areas and a clear sense of privacy inside help balance the activity outside.

Information that is relevant to buyers should never be hidden. Being honest about the work next door usually builds more trust, and stronger offers, than leaving buyers to find out later.

When your home may appeal to developers

If townhouses are being built on similar sections nearby, your land may interest developers and builders too. Whether it does depends on the section's size, shape, slope, access and zoning under the Auckland Unitary Plan. Auckland's intensification rules changed in 2025 and further changes have been proposed, so developers will check the current position themselves before making an offer.

A campaign can speak to both home buyers and developers, but it should not promise a development outcome that has not been confirmed. See selling land or a home with development potential for how these sales work.

Timing your sale

Some sellers wonder whether to sell before nearby townhouses are finished or wait until they are complete. Once a project is finished, buyers can see the final buildings, which removes some uncertainty. But a group of new homes coming onto the market at the same time can add competition for buyers. Neither choice is right for every home, and it is worth weighing both against your own timing.

Frequently asked questions

Do new townhouses nearby lower the value of an older Avondale home?

Not necessarily. New townhouses add choice for buyers, so an older home has to make its own case, but many buyers still want what a standalone home offers: land, privacy, a garden, parking and room to improve. The effect depends on the home, the section and how directly it competes with the new builds. A property-specific appraisal using recent standalone sales is the best way to understand it.

Should I compare my older home with townhouse prices?

Not directly. A standalone home on its own section usually sits in a different buyer pool from a townhouse, so the most relevant evidence is recent sales of similar standalone homes in Avondale. Buyers will still look at townhouse prices, so it helps to understand them, but they should not be used on their own to set the price of an older home.

Do I need to tell buyers about construction next door?

Be upfront about it. Buyers will usually see or hear building work, and many check council records for nearby resource consents. Information that is relevant to buyers should not be hidden, and explaining what is happening next door, as far as you know, usually builds more trust than leaving buyers to find out later.

Could a developer be interested in my older Avondale home?

Possibly, if the section's size, shape, zoning and access suit new housing. Auckland's intensification rules changed in 2025 and further changes have been proposed, so developers will check the current zoning and any overlays themselves. If your land may suit development, it is worth marketing to developers and builders as well as home buyers.

Should I wait until the townhouses next door are finished before selling?

It depends on your timing and the stage of the work. Once a project is finished, buyers can see the final buildings and whether sunlight, privacy or outlook are affected, which can remove uncertainty. But new homes coming onto the market at the same time can add competition. It is worth weighing both before deciding when to list.
