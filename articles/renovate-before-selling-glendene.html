Should You Renovate Before Selling in Glendene?

Many Glendene homes were built between the 1950s and 1970s. They are solid, but the kitchens and bathrooms often show their age. Here is an honest look at whether renovating before you sell is likely to pay off.

By Edita Andrijasevic · Published 26 October 2026 · Updated 26 October 2026

Glendene homeowners often ask whether they should renovate the kitchen or bathroom before they sell. It is a fair question. Much of Glendene's housing dates from the 1950s to the 1970s: solid construction on good-sized sections, but often with original or tired kitchens, bathrooms and finishes. It is natural to wonder whether updating them would bring a better price.

The honest answer is that a full renovation before selling often does not pay for itself in Glendene, and in some cases it can work against you. This guide explains why, and what usually matters more to the buyers who are most likely to make an offer. For local selling support, see the Glendene real estate agent service page.

The quick answer

For most Glendene homes, fixing what is broken, removing buyer uncertainty and presenting the home and section well will do more than a full renovation. A major renovation can cost more than buyers will add to their offers, delay the sale, and push the price beyond what many of Glendene's likeliest buyers can borrow. There are exceptions, so it is worth checking your specific property before spending.

Why this question comes up so often in Glendene

Glendene sits between Glen Eden, Kelston and Henderson. Many of its homes were built in the same period, so a large share of the homes for sale are of a similar age. Buyers walking through open homes in Glendene expect to see some original features. An older kitchen is not a surprise to them; a home that feels neglected or uncertain is.

That changes the question. It is less about whether your kitchen is old and more about whether buyers can see that the house is sound, and whether the price makes sense for its condition compared with other homes they are looking at in Glendene, Glen Eden and Kelston.

Who buys in Glendene, and how each group sees renovation

Different buyers look at the same home in different ways. Knowing which group is most likely for your home is the starting point for deciding what to do before selling.

First-home buyersOften working to a firm lending limit. They want a home that is warm, dry and working, not necessarily new.
RenovatorsLooking for a sound home they can improve. A recent renovation removes the upside they are paying for.
InvestorsFocused on maintenance, rental appeal and Healthy Homes requirements rather than designer finishes.
FamiliesWeighing section size, layout, bedrooms and location alongside condition and price.

Where a full renovation can work against you

It can push the price past the likeliest buyers

Many Glendene buyers have a firm budget set by their lender. If a renovation lifts your asking price above that budget, some of the buyers who would have bought the home in its original condition can no longer make an offer. The buyers who can afford the higher price may also be looking at renovated homes in other suburbs, so you could end up competing in a tougher group.

Renovators lose the reason to buy

Buyers who want to renovate are paying for the chance to add value themselves. When the main work has already been done, and done to someone else's taste, that buyer group often moves on.

The cost is rarely returned in full

New kitchens and bathrooms are expensive, and buyers tend to judge them against their own preferences. Spending on finishes that buyers would have chosen differently is one of the most common ways sellers overcapitalise. The guide to preparing your home for sale covers this in more detail.

It delays the sale and can add risk

Renovations take time, and trades are not always available when you need them. Homes of this age can also hold surprises once work starts. WorkSafe says a building built before 2000 probably contains some asbestos materials, which need to be identified before they are disturbed. Rushed work, or work done without a required building consent, can create the kind of uncertainty buyers dislike most.

What is usually worth doing before you sell

The work that tends to help most is the work that makes buyers feel confident the home has been looked after. In Glendene, that usually means:

  • Fixing what is broken. Dripping taps, sticking doors, cracked tiles, damaged spouting, broken fittings and anything that suggests deferred maintenance.
  • Dealing with moisture and drainage. Address the cause of damp, mould or poor drainage rather than covering it up. Buyers and building inspectors look for it.
  • Freshening, not replacing. Paint touch-ups, a thorough clean, new tapware or handles where needed, and good lighting can make an older kitchen or bathroom look cared for at a fraction of the cost.
  • Presenting the section. Glendene sections are often a big part of the appeal. A tidy lawn, clear paths, sound fencing and usable outdoor areas help buyers picture themselves living there.
  • Warmth for investors and first-home buyers. Insulation, heating and ventilation matter to buyers who plan to rent the home out, and to anyone who wants a warm, dry home.

Edita's home presentation advice focuses on exactly these choices: what to fix, what to present well and what to leave. For vacant or hard-to-picture homes, home staging can sometimes help more than building work.

Remove the uncertainty buyers discount most

Glendene buyers often discount uncertainty more heavily than known, manageable issues. A buyer who knows the roof will need attention in a few years can price that in. A buyer who is unsure whether an extension was consented, or why a corner of the lounge feels damp, may lower their offer by more than the problem is worth, or walk away.

Before the campaign, it is worth gathering:

  • A current LIM, so you know what the council file shows and whether past work was consented.
  • Your title, and for a cross-lease home, the flats plan.
  • Any building consents, code compliance certificates and records of work done.
  • Information about retaining walls, drainage and any known issues, so they can be explained clearly.

Some sellers also commission a pre-sale building report. It can bring problems to light before a buyer finds them, and give buyers more confidence when it is made available to them.

Cross-lease homes: check before you change anything

Glendene has a broad mix of freehold and cross-lease homes. On a cross-lease property, the flats plan shows the footprint of each home. If an alteration changes that footprint, such as an extension, an enclosed deck or a new garage, the plan may no longer match what is built. Buyers' lawyers often check this, and putting it right can take time and may need the other owners' agreement.

If you own a cross-lease home and are planning work before selling, check the flats plan and any consent requirements first. If past alterations are not shown on the plan, it is usually better to know before you list than to find out during a buyer's due diligence.

When renovation can make sense

There are situations where doing more work before selling is worth considering:

  • A half-finished project. Buyers often find an unfinished renovation harder to value than an original room. Finishing it to a sound, neutral standard can help.
  • Something that no longer works. A bathroom that leaks or a kitchen that cannot be used may put off first-home buyers and lenders. Repairing it to a functional standard is different from a full renovation.
  • A clear gap in local sales. If recent Glendene sales show renovated homes like yours selling well above original ones by more than the work would cost, the numbers may support it. This needs checking against real comparable sales, not assumptions.

If your section is large enough that builders may be interested, renovating the existing house may not matter to them at all. See selling land or a home with development potential.

A simple way to decide

For each job you are considering, ask three questions. Will the likeliest buyer for this home value it? Will they add more to their offer than it costs? And is it worth the extra time before the home goes on the market? If the answer to any of them is no, the money is usually better spent on repairs, paperwork and presentation.

Frequently asked questions

Is it worth renovating a kitchen or bathroom before selling in Glendene?

Usually not as a full renovation. Many Glendene buyers are first-home buyers working to a lending limit, or renovators who would rather choose their own finishes. A full kitchen or bathroom renovation can cost more than buyers will add to their offer, and it can push the price above what the likeliest buyers can borrow. Repairing what is broken, cleaning thoroughly and fixing small faults usually does more for the result.

What should I fix before selling my Glendene home?

Start with anything a buyer's building report is likely to pick up: leaks, rot, broken fittings, poor drainage, damaged spouting and obvious maintenance. Then look at presentation: paint touch-ups, a clean and tidy section, clear outdoor areas and good light. Getting your LIM and property documents ready before the campaign also helps buyers feel confident.

Do Glendene buyers prefer a renovated home?

Some do, but many prefer a home that is sound and move-in ready rather than newly renovated. First-home buyers want a home that is warm, dry and working. Renovators and investors often prefer a well-maintained original home they can improve themselves. The right approach depends on which buyer group is most likely for your property.

Can a renovation cause problems when selling a cross-lease home?

It can. If an alteration changes the footprint of a cross-lease home, such as an extension, an enclosed deck or a new garage, the flats plan may no longer match what is built. Buyers' lawyers often check this, and fixing it can take time and may need the other owners' agreement. Check the flats plan and consent requirements before starting any work that changes the building.

How do I know what is worth doing before I sell?

Compare the cost of each job with how buyers are likely to respond to it, and think about how long it will delay the sale. A property-specific appraisal can show where your home sits against recent Glendene sales in its current condition, which makes it easier to decide what to fix, what to present well and what to leave for the next owner.
